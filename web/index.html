The time model learned from this data

Estimated delivery time = fixed minutes + (pickup distance x assumed pickup pace + delivery distance x fitted pace) x traffic x weather x vehicle multipliers. A multiplier of 1.3 means 30% slower than distance alone predicts.

How the optimiser works

Three methods from the course, all written from scratch in NumPy and checked against SciPy. The examples below are solved live by that code.

1. Assignment problem: Hungarian method

Matches each order to exactly one rider to minimise total delivery time.

  1. Subtract the smallest value of each row from that row, then the same for each column.
  2. Cover all zeros with the fewest possible lines.
  3. If the number of lines equals the matrix size, choose one zero in each row and column: that is the optimal assignment.
  4. Otherwise subtract the smallest uncovered value from uncovered cells, add it to cells covered twice, and repeat.

With more orders than riders we add dummy riders; an order matched to a dummy is not served in this batch.

2. Transportation problem: Vogel's method and MODI

Decides how many riders to move from zones with spare riders to zones with many orders, using the least total distance.

  1. Balance supply and demand with a dummy zone if the totals differ.
  2. Vogel's Approximation: for each row and column take the gap between the two smallest costs, pick the largest gap, and fill its cheapest cell.
  3. MODI: compute u and v with u + v = cost on every filled cell, then the opportunity cost of every empty cell.
  4. If every opportunity cost is at least zero, the plan is optimal. Otherwise shift flow around the closed loop of the most negative cell and repeat.

3. Linear programming: Simplex (Two-Phase and Big-M)

Plans which zone-to-zone deliveries to serve so that profit = revenue - rider cost - late penalty is as large as possible, subject to rider capacity, a longest allowed trip, and a minimum share of orders served in each zone.

  1. Add slack variables to the "at most" rows. Rows with "at least" need surplus and artificial variables.
  2. Two-Phase: Phase I minimises the artificial variables to find a feasible start; Phase II then optimises profit.
  3. Big-M: artificial variables get a huge penalty M in the objective, so one phase is enough.
  4. Each iteration brings in the most promising variable, picks the leaving row by the minimum ratio test, and pivots until no improvement is left.

Assumptions

  • The dataset has no live rider positions. Riders wait near real restaurants of the chosen city with about 1 km of random spread.
  • All times in the comparison are model estimates, not measurements.
  • Revenue, rider cost, the late penalty and the 30-minute promise are assumptions, because the data has no prices.
  • The Kaggle "City" column is only the city type. The real city is read from the rider ID.
